Why Congo?

Congo's land has been blessed, but also cursed with an abundance of resources. The Kantaga province is especially rich in minerals , such as gold, cobalt, and iron. The mineral deposits that sit in almost every corner of the country are deemed to be the richest in the continent. Congo's economy, therefore, relies heavily on the extraction and exportation of these minerals. Over 90% of the country's exports are minerals harvested from the mines of Congo. Although Congo's exports are all sought after, cobalt remains as the most desired resource. Most lithium ion batteries - a type of rechargeable battery that is most commonly used in electronic devices - require cobalt as an essential ingredient. An average electric vehicle requires 13kgs of Cobalt, while a mobile phone typically requires around 7 grams per device. As phone usage skyrockets and electric vehicles are advertised as the more "environmentally conscious" option, the demands for cobalt are higher than ever. It is expected to reach 222,000 tonnes by 2025, which is triple the amount just fifteen years ago.

Congo alone is responsible for more than 70% of the world's cobalt production currently. Foreign countries have been investing in Congo, building mines, and taking the resources that were gathered by Congolese hands. One of its main investors is China, who has recently signed a mining deal worth over 6 billion USD, and claims about 80% of the cobalt produced in the country. Mega-corporations such as Apple have also been accused of using cobalt that was gathered in the mines of Congo, although they have been denying the accusations.

When did it begin?

Although the demand for Congo’s resources may seem recent, the exploitation of its resources goes much deeper into the history of the country. Much like many other countries in the continent, Congo faces the direct, long lasting effects of colonization and imperialism. Congo, before the 16th century, was one of the wealthiest kingdoms in Africa due to its richness of resources. The Kingdom of Kongo dominated the western region of the continent. Towards the end of the 15th century, European sailors, specifically the Portuguese, started to enter the kingdom. They soon realized the abundance of minerals and other resources, and began to plan a way to monopolize it. The Portuguese destroyed any indigenous policies that had the potential of disrupting their trade, slaughtering elites and kings as they carried out their plan. By the 16th century, the kingdom was merely a leaderless piece of land, and a dock for European managed trade.

The stifled development of the country made it easier for other forces to claim power over it. By the 19th century, Belgium entered the scene. Belgium, seeking opportunities for rubber export and trade, began committing atrocities in the region. The Congolese people were forced to submit under the control of Belgians. Disobedience was met by brutal violence, which often included flogging, severing of hands, and death. Once the world wars unfolded, western forces rushed to Congo for resources for their weapons, and mining boomed. Because the infrastructure and development of the country was kept at a barely functioning level, independence in 1960 resulted in disaster. Belgian forces still remained in the country, with only 3 out of the 5000 positions in the Congolese government being held by the people of Congo. Backed by western forces, tyrants were able to keep their position in the government, as long as they didn’t affect the flow of minerals out of the country. As a result of the greed for minerals, Congo is no longer the wealthy kingdom it used to be.

Internal conflicts between ethnic groups and the constant control of Belgium left the country in total dysfunction, while corporations and countries who once benefited from the suffering of the Congolese still thrive from its resources.

What's happening to the Congolese people?

With the collapsed economy, over 70% of the population lives in extreme poverty. Many of them have no choice but to work at the cobalt mines to make any sort of livelihood. The working conditions in the cobalt mines of Congo are extremely dire. The miners work excessive hours during the day, with little to no equipment provided. Most of them work without any sort of safety precautions, even though they work up to 100 meters under the ground in the tunnels. As a result, many of them experience health deterioration due to radiation exposure, inhaling dust and toxic chemicals, and exhaustion. Since the country cannot provide basic medical care for many of its people, the miners often succumb to their injuries and health complications. Many Congolese workers also reported seeing or experiencing abuse from the supervisors on the scene. Miners were subjected to physical violence, such as being kicked, shoved, hit, poked with a stick, and slapped. They also face verbal degradation, insults, racist remarks, and other kinds of mental abuse. Despite these horrifying working conditions, many workers earn a devastatingly small amount of pay: 2 dollars per day.

Mine workers are not the only people subjected to appalling situations. Forced evictions and village destructions caused by mine expansions are prevalent in the country. Many former residents are forced to evacuate the area without even having the chance to grab their belongings. Without any alternatives, families are left to sleep in the jungles and forests. The evictions that occur also tend to be violent by nature. Any villager who tries to stop the destruction is often beaten and attacked. Houses are set on fire, sometimes when people are still in them. Farmlands are also targeted in destruction for mine expansion. Many farmers are evicted from their farmland without any sort of discussion or prior notice. The majority are not given any compensations or alternatives, and lose their only source of income. As a result, families struggle to sustain their lives, living under extreme malnutrition and hunger.

Women have also reported being sexually assaulted by the soldiers who carry out these evictions. One woman from the a farmland near the village of Tshamundenda recalls being gang-raped by a group of soliders. Although she was forcibly impregnated, she was unable to receive proper care and medical support after the incident.
